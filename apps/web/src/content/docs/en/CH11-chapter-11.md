# Directing: Intention, Communication, and Decision-Making

> Editorial state: research draft; English working translation. Theory, terminology, film case, bilingual text, and figures have not received publication approval.

<a id="CH11-S00"></a>
## Scope and evidence labels
This chapter examines how a director turns dramatic intentions in a script into choices that can be rehearsed, photographed, edited, and examined by a team. The central task is not to issue technical orders to every department. It is to establish a shared purpose, ask clear questions, understand specialist feedback, and make traceable decisions among story, time, budget, safety, rights, and performance needs. This chapter focuses on directing practice; it does not replace the detailed study of actor movement (CH12), staging (CH13), or storyboarding and previsualization (CH14).

Labels distinguish the status of claims: **verified fact** means a source supports the statement; **theory** identifies a film-studies context; **practice** is a method that may be adapted to a production; **AI hypothesis** is a proposed use that still needs testing; and **demonstration** is an original exercise, not historical evidence. DGA material describes specific union agreements or voluntary guidance. It does not establish the same rules for every jurisdiction, employment relationship, production scale, or contract.[R078][R079]

<a id="CH11-S01"></a>
## Learning objectives and audience
By the end of this chapter, readers should be able to:

1. Convert an abstract theme or directing intention into a scene objective, character action, observable audience evidence, and production choices that can be discussed.
2. Distinguish between explaining an intention, offering a reference, proposing options, and making a decision, then record the decision according to the production’s roles and agreements.
3. Prepare different forms of communication for actors, camera, design, sound, editing, and producing instead of treating every collaborator as the same kind of executor.
4. Gather feedback during rehearsal, production, and editing, and identify which changes serve the story and which affect continuity, schedule, resources, or rights.
5. Treat AI-generated directions, storyboard sketches, or text analysis as proposals to verify, identifying their assumptions, omissions, and decisions that cannot be delegated to a model.

**Audience:** beginning directors, short-film and student crews, screenwriters, producers, cinematography, design, editing, and image-making teams using generative AI in development. Titles and authority vary across markets and production types; adapt this chapter’s workflow to the actual team agreement. **Assessed outputs:** a one-page directing brief, a scene handoff sheet, a decision record with rationale and scope, and a previsualization proposal that labels AI assumptions.

<a id="CH11-S02"></a>
## Prerequisites
Readers should be familiar with scripts, scenes, beats, shot size, camera shots, composition, editorial continuity, and basic production roles. Chapters CH01–CH10 provide useful context, especially CH02 and CH03 for readers new to film language. These are recommendations rather than entry requirements; essential directing concepts are restated in the case study.

Directing also requires three non-equipment skills. First, listening and questioning so that writers, performers, and department heads can explain what they need. Second, recognizing reversibility: some choices can be tested again during rehearsal, while others commit expensive sets, visual effects, locations, or shooting days. Third, explaining trade-offs so collaborators understand how a decision responds to the story objective. These are practical recommendations, not universal job standards.

**Safety boundary:** do not use humiliation, threats, nonconsensual physical contact, or dangerous behavior to “draw out” a truthful performance. Intimacy, hazardous action, children, animals, stunts, and other high-risk scenes should be planned under applicable local law, production policies, and qualified professional guidance. This chapter is not legal or occupational-safety advice.

## Core concepts and professional terms
The following working definitions may vary across regions and remain subject to bilingual professional review. Term IDs are stable cross-chapter references:

- [TERM-CH11-DIRECTOR](#CH11-S03): director. In a particular production arrangement, the person who integrates story, performance, audiovisual form, and collaboration; the title does not imply unrestricted final authority over every worker, budget, or version.[R077][R078]
- [TERM-CH11-DIRECTORIAL-INTENTION](#CH11-S03): directing intention. A discussable hypothesis about what the audience should understand, feel, or anticipate in a scene; it should connect to concrete action or audiovisual evidence rather than adjectives alone.[R077]
- [TERM-CH11-DIRECTING-BRIEF](#CH11-S03): a document organizing story objectives, scene constraints, shared language, open questions, and department needs so collaborators can respond; it is not a list of aesthetic commands for every department.
- [TERM-CH11-BLOCKING](#CH11-S03): actor blocking. The arrangement of performers’ movement in relation to one another and the camera; blocking and shot design influence one another. Detailed techniques appear in CH12 and CH13.[R077]
- [TERM-CH11-SHOT-LIST](#CH11-S03): shot list. A working list of shots planned for scenes and shooting days; it should serve the story and production conditions without replacing judgment on set.[R077]
- [TERM-CH11-DEPARTMENT-HANDOFF](#CH11-S03): department handoff. Translating a creative objective into information each department can evaluate, develop, or execute while preserving its specialist methods.
- [TERM-CH11-DECISION-LOG](#CH11-S03): decision log. A record of consequential choices with date, question, options, rationale, responsible role, impact, and conditions for reopening; it is not a verbatim meeting transcript.
- [TERM-CH11-DIRECTORS-CUT](#CH11-S03): director’s cut. In a particular production or contract context, a version or rights arrangement for a director’s editorial stage; DGA provisions have a defined scope and should not be generalized to other productions.[R078]

## History and principles
**Verified facts and theoretical context:** the director’s work has not always matched today’s familiar idea of one author unifying an entire film. Rutgers University Press’s *Directing* traces changes in directing as an art and profession from the silent era onward, emphasizing how production institutions, creative divisions, industrial conditions, and collaboration shape the job.[R085] A Library of Congress overview of early American film also describes a shift from camera operators taking on multiple production tasks to writing, directing, acting, cinematography, editing, and design becoming distinct crafts. This is an overview of U.S. film history, not a universal account.[R086]

After the 1950s, auteur theory encouraged closer attention to recognizable style and themes across directors’ films. Later scholarship continued to debate film authorship, industrial conditions, and creative work shared across departments. Oxford Bibliographies identifies the artistic claims of other film workers and the role of collaboration in constructing film authorship as continuing research issues; Rutgers’ history likewise foregrounds collaboration, compromise, and industry conditions.[R084][R085] This chapter therefore treats a director’s integrative responsibility as an analytical and practical model, not as a “lone genius” account of filmmaking.

**Principle:** a director translates an idea that cannot be photographed directly—such as “the character no longer trusts the other person”—into testable action, information gaps, spatial distance, eyelines, sound, and editing rhythm, then invites specialists to propose workable solutions. Intention is a hypothesis for the team, not an answer. If rehearsal or a rough cut does not convey the expected relationship, the director should revisit the hypothesis, performance task, camera viewpoint, or scene information rather than simply repeat abstract adjectives.

<a id="CH11-S05"></a>
## Traditional production practice
AFI’s introduction to directing identifies overall creative direction, work with actors, and collaboration with department heads such as cinematography, design, editing, writing, and producing as central aspects of the role.[R077] Directing is not limited to saying “action” and “cut” on set. Decisions develop through script analysis, casting, location scouting, rehearsal, visual planning, shooting plans, and postproduction. DGA materials also describe creative participation across preparation, production, and postproduction within the scope of specific member agreements; those rights apply only to covered work and are not universal legal rules.[R078]

**Practical workflow (adapt to scale):**

1. **Read the script and identify the central question:** break a scene down by character objective, obstacle, choice, and consequence; list unresolved story questions before choosing equipment.
2. **Establish shared intention:** briefly describe what the audience should know or feel differently before and after the scene, then list observable evidence.
3. **Discuss with the writer and producer:** confirm the script version, creative scope, time, cost, rights, schedule, and information that must remain. Actual authority depends on the relevant agreements.
4. **Rehearse with performers:** give objectives, the other character’s behavior, scene facts, and playable tasks. Invite alternatives and use rehearsal to test the writing. Do not make a psychological diagnosis or emotion label the only direction.
5. **Brief departments:** camera, design, costume, sound, editing, and producing need different questions and deliverables. Invite department heads to propose alternatives and identify risks.
6. **Plan the shoot:** compare scene breakdown, blocking, shot list, schedule, transport, props, continuity, sound, and safety. Responsibilities among the director, first assistant director, cinematographer, and other heads depend on project practice.
7. **Verify on set:** check whether performance, image, and sound convey the objective; log unresolved issues and possible pickups. Communicate on-set changes to affected departments.
8. **Review in post:** use dailies, rough cuts, screenings, or other appropriate methods to test whether the audience can follow the information and emotional turn; record the version and reasons for changes.

DGA’s Code of Preferred Practices explicitly describes itself as voluntary guidance. It includes recommendations such as involving the director early and avoiding unnecessary reductions to preparation or editing time; these are not rules for every production.[R079]

## Technical change and limits in the AI era
**Practice and AI hypotheses to test:** a language model may help turn a directing brief into a question list, suggest alternative readings of a scene, check documents for missing fields, or organize approved scene information into department handoffs. These are workflow hypotheses and prompt examples. They do not assert that any particular product has the same features or that a model understands performance, political context, culture, or a director’s intention. Before relying on a tool, verify its current official documentation, data terms, and version, then test it with project material.

Generated images, storyboards, or previsualizations may help a team discuss composition, scene direction, and alternatives. Yet they may alter a character’s appearance, ignore location continuity, invent props, or reproduce risky visual features. Generated output is not an approved performer, set, shot, safety plan, or final illustration. For real performer images, scripts, confidential pitches, or client data, confirm production authorization and review service terms and retention practices before use; do not paste confidential material into an unapproved service.

A safer loop is: “human sets the objective—model proposes—team questions—physical rehearsal or technical test—human decides—version is recorded.” Label model output as a draft and preserve the input version, generation date, model/tool version when available, human edits, and adoption rationale. A model cannot replace consent, ethical judgment, performance boundaries, or on-set safety responsibility. Do not use AI facial-emotion classification or text inference to diagnose a performer’s mental state.

## Step-by-step workflow
The following is a working model for prep meetings on short films, advertising, documentaries, or features. It is not a statutory standard. Add the relevant review for documentary ethics, brand approval, union terms, or location policies where applicable.

1. **Freeze input versions:** identify script revision, page or scene IDs, references, and decision-makers. Notes with an unverified version are not current instructions.
2. **Write the scene proposition:** in one or two sentences, state what a character wants, what opposes them, and what relationship or information changes by the end.
3. **Propose audience evidence:** list evidence observable in image, sound, performance, or editing. Add an alternative if the audience does not understand it.
4. **Rehearse and listen:** let performers try the playable task; record useful actions, timing, pauses, and discoveries instead of fixing every reaction in advance.
5. **Collect department proposals:** ask each department for one option that serves the intention, one risk or resource note, and one viable alternative.
6. **Review constraints:** check safety, consent, location, budget, schedule, continuity, sound, rights, accessibility, and postproduction needs.
7. **Choose and record:** note the chosen option, rejected alternatives and reasons, decision-maker, affected scenes/departments, documents to update, and conditions for reopening the decision.
8. **Refocus on set:** confirm the day’s story objective and must-capture information in shared language. Notify affected leads of any immediate change.
9. **Review material:** with the editor, first test information clarity, performance continuity, viewpoint, and audiovisual relationships, then discuss stylistic details.
10. **Archive the version:** version the latest brief, shot list, continuity records, and decision log together so old instructions do not circulate on set.

## Prompt and technical parameters
The model-neutral prompt below requests proposals for human discussion. It does not ask a model to make the final directing decision. Independently verify any film fact, performer information, production specification, contract, or rights claim in its output.

**Prompt template: turn directing intention into department discussion proposals**

    Role: Preproduction research assistant.
    Authority: Not the director or approver.
    Inputs: Approved script version and scene summary only.
    Do not invent facts, equipment, locations, or tool features.
    Objective: [goal / obstacle / ending change]
    Intention: [audience knowledge / feeling / expectation]
    Limits: [duration / location / cast / budget / schedule]
    Safety: [consent boundaries]
    Task: Propose three distinct approaches for discussion.
    For each, list evidence, performer task, camera/design questions,
    sound/edit possibilities, risks, and human decisions.
    Mark assumptions “unverified”. Ask about missing information.
    Include script version and scene ID. Return Markdown or JSON.
    End with inputs, model proposals, and unverified items.
  
  **Reproducibility fields:** tool/model version, date, prompt version, input-data hash, available settings such as temperature or seed, output hash, human edits, and adoption/rejection rationale. Services do not expose the same controls; do not assume every model supports temperature, seed, fixed versions, or reproducible output. Write “not tested” when no test was run.

## Film case study
### *The Godfather* (1972): translating dramatic intention into audiovisual layers

**Verified film-record facts:** AFI Catalog credits Francis Ford Coppola as director; Mario Puzo and Coppola as writers; Gordon Willis as cinematographer; and Dean Tavoularis for production design. The record also summarizes the film’s relationship to Puzo’s novel and production history.[R082] These catalog records verify credits and traceable context; they cannot by themselves prove a creator’s private intention.

**Sourced production/form description:** an American Cinematographer reprint of Gordon Willis’s June 1971 interview describes his approach to keeping the film mechanically simple and retaining the qualities of a location; the interview also records a shared direction with Coppola.[R083] A separate ASC article describes the opening monologue by Bonasera: the camera moves slowly back from a tight close-up, gradually revealing the Corleone office and Don’s profile. It notes that the directors had established a “no zooms” rule, which was suspended once, and connects that choice with introducing the character and delaying information.[R081] These are descriptions in published sources, not a frame-by-frame viewing record of a particular restoration. This draft supplies no timecode.

**Analysis for this chapter:** “power is not fully visible at first” is a possible directing-intention hypothesis. The team can test how focal distance, disclosure timing, visibility of light, entrances, and speech rhythm jointly shape the audience’s viewpoint. This is an interpretation of the cited material, not the only valid reading. It also does not assign the cinematographer’s work to the director alone. The lesson is that a formal exception can be justified by a shared objective—not that one should “never zoom” or that “darkness always means power.”

**Original transformation exercise:** invent a different story: an apartment manager receives an urgent request from a tenant at night. Borrow only the abstract operation “let the audience hear a request before gradually revealing the status and surroundings of the person receiving it.” Do not copy the Corleone office, characters, costumes, composition, performers, or film shots. Teams make a locked-off version, a sound-first version, and a reveal-through-movement version. Compare how quickly viewers understand the situation, power relationship, and performance space; record how the new design becomes its own. Do not reproduce recognizable scenes under the label “homage.”

**Viewing task:** an editor or author should identify a lawful viewing version, release/restoration details, viewing source, observer, and timecode, then add a first-hand viewing record. If a lawful version is unavailable, retain the published-source analysis and exclude new shot-level claims.

## Common errors and quality checks
| Common error | Observable problem | Correction and acceptance check |
|---|---|---|
| Intention is only adjectives | “More cinematic” or “sadder” does not point to scene action | State character action, information change, and audiovisual evidence; ask different departments to paraphrase |
| Preference is mistaken for story need | A favored shot or device overrides the scene objective | Compare at least two approaches and state the gains and losses of each |
| Performer receives only an emotion label | The performer is asked to imitate an expression without a playable task | Reframe as an objective, partner behavior, obstacle, tactic, or concrete event; review in rehearsal |
| Director assumes everyone received the change | Departments work from different script or brief versions | Check filename, revision, change marks, and recipients; identify who logs on-set changes |
| Meeting decision has no reopening condition | The team cannot trace the rationale when a constraint changes | Record rationale, impact, deadline, and a trigger for reconsideration |
| AI output is treated as fact or approval | Sources, feasibility, safety, consent, or rights have not been checked | Label each model proposal; ask the responsible lead to verify; keep unverified items out of call sheets and final assets |
| Too many design elements compete | Costume, camera, sound, and lighting pull attention in different directions | Run a subtraction test against the objective; remove one element and check whether audience understanding worsens |

**Chapter-level QA:** Is the script version and scene ID clear? Can the character objective and audience information be stated? Can each handoff become a concrete question? Are safety and consent boundaries assigned to qualified people? Does each choice include rationale, version, owner, and downstream impact? Is AI output labeled unverified? Does the case source support only the scope of its description? Any “no” should send the work back for preparation; this checklist does not replace professional judgment on set.

## Exercises, assessment, and further research
### Exercise: directing-intention work package

Choose a self-written or licensed one-to-two-page scene. Do not use an unlicensed full screenplay. In a team:

1. Write a scene proposition of no more than 80 words; list the character objective, obstacle, change, and information the audience needs.
2. Write two different playable directions for performers and explain how the process respects their choices and safety boundaries.
3. Make a one-page directing brief with intention, audience evidence, a written description of tone/rhythm references, and questions for departments to solve together.
4. Give camera, design, sound, editing, and producing one concrete question each; ask a classmate in each role to suggest an option and risk.
5. Compare two scene approaches through rehearsal or paper previsualization; write a decision log and one condition that would reopen the decision.
6. If using AI, save the prompt and output hash, label unverified claims, and identify at least three model suggestions that a human revised or rejected.

### Assessment rubric (0–2 points per item; 10 total)

- **Story legibility:** 0 no clear objective; 1 objective exists but evidence is vague; 2 objective, obstacle, and change are observable in the scene.
- **Actionable communication:** 0 adjectives only; 1 some departments receive clear questions; 2 multiple departments can propose specialist solutions to a shared intention.
- **Collaboration and performance ethics:** 0 no listening or safety boundary; 1 mentioned but not integrated; 2 includes rehearsal feedback, consent/safety checks, and an owner.
- **Decision traceability:** 0 no version or rationale; 1 options are recorded without impacts; 2 records version, rationale, affected roles, follow-ups, and reopening conditions.
- **AI literacy and sources:** 0 model output treated as fact; 1 partial checking; 2 separates input, model proposal, sourced fact, and unverified material.

The score evaluates this work package, not a film’s artistic quality. Further research may compare directing authority across production systems, the division of labor from silent to sound cinema, auteur theory and collective authorship, and how AI previsualization changes participation by performers and departments. Include perspectives across countries, languages, industries, gender, and race rather than treating Hollywood as a universal model.
