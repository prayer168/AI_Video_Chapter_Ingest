# CH07 | Character, Desire, Conflict, and Character Arcs
> Status: research draft. Traditional Chinese is the working source text; this English version is a working translation. Both require professional editorial and author review. Film examples draw on published criticism; this project does not claim that its author viewed a specific edition or verified timecodes.

<a id="CH07-S01"></a>
## Learning objectives
By the end of this chapter, readers can:

1. Express a character's observable action, goal, resistance, and cost as a testable causal chain.
2. Distinguish character information, characterization, and a character's narrative function without reducing a person to trait labels.
3. Design an external goal, an inner need or belief, strategies and counterstrategies, and explain how choices create conflict.
4. Describe change, refusal to change, or a stable arc through action and choice rather than a concluding declaration alone.
5. Use AI to propose and compare character options while reserving human decisions about causality, culture, performance, and ethics.

<a id="CH07-S02"></a>
## Prerequisites
CH06, “Story, Plot, and Causal Structure,” is recommended for its account of how events connect through actions and consequences. Readers need not accept rules such as “the protagonist must change” or “every character needs a full biography.” This chapter treats them as optional strategies, not universal laws.

|---|---|---|---|
| TERM-CH07-CHARACTER | 角色 | Character | A figure recognized and constructed through cues such as body, social position, action, emotion, traits, or thought. The dimensions used depend on the work; they do not amount to a complete real-world personality. |
| TERM-CH07-CHARACTERIZATION | 人物塑造 | Characterization | The process by which a work invites viewers to infer a person through action, reaction, appearance, voice, relationships, and information. |
| TERM-CH07-GOAL | 角色目標 | Character goal | A describable outcome a character seeks, avoids, or completes in a particular situation; progress should be observable through action and result. |
| TERM-CH07-DESIRE | 欲望／戲劇需求 | Desire / dramatic need | This chapter uses “desire” for a pursuit that drives action. | TERM-CH07-OBSTACLE | 阻力 | Obstacle | An external condition, another person's intention, an institutional limit, or the character's own strategy that prevents direct achievement of a goal. |
| TERM-CH07-CONFLICT | 衝突 | Conflict | Incompatible goals, values, or actions meet in a concrete situation, forcing a choice and producing observable consequences. An argument or raised voice alone does not necessarily create effective dramatic conflict. |
| TERM-CH07-CHARACTER-ARC | 角色弧線 | Character arc | Change in a character's belief, strategy, relationship, or pattern of action across a story. An arc may also involve deterioration, recurrence, refusal to change, or stability; every story does not require positive growth. |
| TERM-CH07-CHARACTER-AGENCY | 角色能動性 | Character agency | The degree to which a character makes choices under constraint and affects subsequent events. Constraint does not mean an absence of agency; identify structural limits and the costs of available choices. |

These are editorial working definitions; they remain drafts pending terminology review. Bordwell's analysis suggests reading characters through dimensions such as body, social role, capacity for action, traits, emotions, and thoughts; narration also establishes relative importance and contrasts among characters (R052). Field's “dramatic need” belongs to a particular screenwriting method, not to psychological diagnosis or a sole theory (R053).

## History and principles
**Theoretical context.** Aristotle's *Poetics* discusses action, plot, character, and other components of tragedy. Translations of terms such as *ethos* and *praxis*, as well as chapter and page locators, vary by edition. This chapter does not equate classical prescriptions for tragedy with universal rules for modern screenwriting; the adopted edition, translation, and page references remain to be reviewed (R046).

**A practical tradition in dramatic writing.** Lajos Egri's *The Art of Dramatic Writing* treats character, premise, and conflict as a connected set for analyzing dramatic writing. Publisher metadata and the table of contents confirm sections on Character, Character Growth, The Antagonist, and Conflict; this draft does not attach unverified page-specific claims to those sections (R054). Syd Field's public Q&A describes “dramatic need” as what a character wants to achieve and suggests revealing it through incidents and scenes. This usage is not necessarily the same as the “inner need” used by other creative traditions (R053).

**Principles of film narrative.** A character is more than a background sheet. Viewers infer goals, emotions, traits, and relationships from limited cues; actions that produce consequences also enter the causal chain. Character hierarchy, difference, and affinity can help viewers distinguish relationships, but fixed archetypes cannot replace analysis of specific behavior (R052).

**Change is not a mandatory formula.** An arc can involve change, deterioration, stability, recurrence, or the disclosure of a dimension the audience did not previously know. A writer may use an external goal to expose an inner belief, or let a character affect other people or social conditions without changing. These are practical categories used here, not a claim to a single accepted model.

<a id="CH07-S05"></a>
## Traditional production practice
1. **Interview or character file:** First define information that matters within the story period: what the character wants now, what they fear losing, what constrains them, and what cost they will accept. Keep only background that affects choice, performance, or production in the main file; store the rest as research notes.
2. **Goal and strategy breakdown:** Use playable verbs for each scene, such as “persuade,” “delay,” “conceal,” or “test.” A strategy must be performable or filmable; an abstract adjective such as “brave” needs behavioral evidence.
3. **Relationship web and opposition:** For important characters, record compatible or conflicting goals, dependence, power differences, shared information, and relationship changes. Opposition can come from a person, institution, environment, time, or the character's own choices; an antagonist need not be simply evil.
4. **Scene beats and performance:** For each scene, the writer marks the character's entering intention, strategy, the other person's response, a strategy shift, and the leaving state. Director and actors then shape performance together through the text, rehearsal, framing, editing, and sound. Do not turn an internal label directly into an acting instruction.
5. **Script revision:** Check whether a character gains information, changes position, or makes a mistake only because the author needs it. Supply an intelligible route for information, pressure, decision, and cost. A reaction can be contradictory, but viewers need recognizable cues.

## AI-era workflow and limits
AI can help create character cards, list incompatible goals, propose scene-strategy variants, compare a character's beginning and ending states, or check names and knowledge states in structured data. It cannot reliably infer a “true psychology” from a short synopsis, and it cannot replace a screenwriter's judgment about culture, class, disability, trauma, representation, or performance ethics. Generated results may mistake familiar genre conventions for natural causality or give different characters the same voice.

**Suggested data fields (a custom schema, not an industry standard):** `character_id`, `story_role`, `public_goal`, `private_goal`, `belief_before`, `belief_after_or_unchanged`, `constraints`, `resources`, `relationships`, `knowledge_by_scene`, `strategies`, `costs`, `arc_evidence`, `open_questions`, and `source_or_invention_status`. Do not send sensitive personal data to an unapproved service. If a character is based on a real person, address consent, anonymization, and data minimization first.

**Working hypothesis for AI use:** Asking a model for mutually distinct strategies for one character, then having the author choose based on scene facts, may be easier to evaluate than asking it to “write a fully rounded character.” This is a hypothesis for project testing, not an established capability of AI systems.

## Step-by-step workflow
| Step | Action | Output and acceptance test |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Define dramatic scope | State the story period, narrative viewpoint, character function, and central situation. | Each protagonist has a defined story scope; unshown biography is not treated as fact. |
| 2. Write an observable goal | Use “character + action + object/outcome + deadline or condition.” | Progress can be judged from action rather than assertion alone. |
| 3. List constraints and costs | Separate external obstacles, resource limits, institutional pressure, and the character's chosen strategies. | Every constraint connects to a scene or world rule; mark its source or demonstration status. |
| 4. Establish character differences | Compare major characters' goals, methods, information, power, and value priorities. | At least one specific affinity and one conflict exist; differences do not rest only on appearance labels. |
| 5. Design choice points | For each major point, write available options, expected outcomes, and actual costs. | A turn follows a choice or a clearly identified external force, not an uncaused reversal. |
| 6. Describe an arc hypothesis | Compare beliefs, strategies, relationships, and behavioral evidence at the beginning, middle, and end. | Identify change, deterioration, recurrence, refusal, or stability; if evidence is absent, mark it as unresolved. |
| 7. Shape scene actions | For each scene, write intention, strategy, resistance, response, and result. | Emotional meaning can remain ambiguous while scene action and information flow stay legible to the crew. |
| 8. Compare AI variants | Provide only necessary, non-sensitive story information; request constrained fields, alternatives, and reasons. | Preserve prompt, model/version if available, date, human selections, and rejected alternatives. |
| 9. Rehearse or table-read | Ask actors to play performable verbs; record problems with lines, pauses, actions, or relationship comprehension. | Generated text is not treated as an acting order; director and actors adjust together. |
| 10. Revise and track | Update the beat sheet, script, and character-knowledge table with accepted decisions. | Changes are traceable; check downstream scene dependencies. |

## Prompt and technical parameters
The following is a model-neutral **demonstration template**, not a tested feature or fixed parameter for any vendor. There is no universal temperature, token count, or best model setting; record tests against the actual tool and version.

```text
Task: Analyze action and causality in the fictional story below; do not decide canon for the author.
Input: premise, what the character knows in this scene, scene goal, constraints, previous-scene result.
Return a JSON array. Each option contains:
  character_id, immediate_goal, strategy (playable verb), obstacle,
  choice, likely_consequence, what_must_be_true, stereotype_risk,
  evidence_from_input, uncertainties.
Constraints: propose 3 distinct options that fit the known information; do not add biography as fact.
Label each inference as “stated in source,” “inference,” or “new proposal.” Do not output sensitive real-person data.
```

Acceptance checks: JSON parses; IDs are valid; any story fact absent from the input is marked as invention; the three strategies differ materially; character knowledge remains consistent; the writer records why each option was accepted, revised, or rejected. Do not score with untestable phrases such as “more layered” or “more relatable” alone.

## Case study: how goals and actions shape our reading of characters
These are **secondary-source case studies** based on named published criticism. No viewing edition or timecode is recorded for this project; the cases do not claim shot-by-shot observation or edition comparison.

### Case A | *House of Games* (David Mamet, 1987)

Shonni Enelow's essay in the Criterion Collection argues that the film's performance style can be understood through concrete scene objectives, and analyzes Dr. Margaret Ford's defensive posture, loss of control, and renewed assertion of self. This is the critic's interpretation, not the only possible reading. Teaching exercise: replace “she is cold” with observable behavior; ask whom she is trying to influence, what strategy she uses, how the other person responds, and whether later actions change our inference about her (R055).

**Transformative exercise:** Do not copy a film character or scene. Create a new character with professional authority who tries to control a public conversation; design a response that makes the character's initial strategy fail.

### Case B | *The Power of the Dog* (Jane Campion, 2021)

Amy Taubin's Criterion essay notes that the opening voice-over asks what it means to be a man and to protect one's mother, treating that question as a dramatic tension that shadows the film. The essay also interprets character relations, socialized masculinity, and the resolution. These are the critic's readings, not a sole psychological diagnosis of a character (R056). Teaching exercise: separate a character's self-description, social expectations, visible actions, and others' responses; identify when the audience receives support or counterevidence, rather than treating silence or violence as a single trait label.

**Transformative exercise:** With new characters, cultural context, and objects, write a scene about how a person understands an identity demand. Do not imitate the film's family relations, ranch setting, dialogue, or visual design.

### Case C | Dimensions and hierarchy of characters

In “Three Dimensions of Film Narrative,” Bordwell describes cues through which film characters are constructed, including body, social role, capacity for voluntary action, traits, emotions, and thoughts. He also discusses character hierarchy and contrasts. The framework helps analyze how a film distributes character information; it does not require every character to fill every category (R052, “Three Dimensions of Film Narrative,” printed pp. 31–32. Page numbers refer to the printed pagination visible in the author-hosted PDF and must be checked against the adopted edition before publication).

## Common errors and quality checks
| Error | Why it causes problems | Check or repair |
|---|---|---|
| Treating desire, need, and goal as synonyms | Screenwriting traditions define them differently. | Attribute each source's wording; keep Field's “dramatic need” separate from other schools' “inner need.” |
| Using “brave,” “kind,” or “crazy” instead of action | Labels cannot be rehearsed or tested and may reproduce bias. | Support character inferences with specific actions, reactions, and consequences. |
| Giving the protagonist no agency | Plot movement can feel arbitrary. | Check for choices to act, refuse, delay, misjudge, or change strategy; show how structural limits constrain options. |
| Building conflict only on misunderstanding or withheld information | Conflict may vanish when information is revealed, making behavior feel contrived. | Ask whether goals or values remain incompatible if everyone knows the facts. |
| Forcing a positive arc | One cultural idea of growth becomes a universal formula. | Name the chosen arc and its thematic cost; stability or tragedy can also work. |
| Overloading backstory | Biography consumes script space without changing present choices. | Mark each detail as presented in the story or author-only notes, and state its causal use. |
| Making AI-generated characters sound alike | Models may repeat common phrasing, tropes, or stereotypes. | Table-read separately; test vocabulary against goals and pragmatic strategies, not phonetic accent spelling. |
| Using trauma, illness, or identity as a shortcut | Representation may stigmatize or lack context. | Seek review from people with relevant experience or qualified consultants; document research and narrative responsibility. |

Machines can check required fields, JSON schema, character IDs, naming consistency, scene goals and outcomes, and existence of citation IDs. Humans must judge credible action, performance meaning, cultural stereotypes, emotional effect, and persuasive arcs. Passing a machine test does not prove successful characterization.

## Exercises, assessment, and further research
### Exercise 1 | Goal, strategy, and cost

Invent a situation of no more than 150 words in which a character wants an ordinary but important object. List the goal, two strategies, obstacle, the other person's goal, the character's choice, and its cost. Score each item 0–2: observable goal; conflict beyond a mere argument; choice causing traceable consequences; no inference presented as established backstory.

### Exercise 2 | Arc evidence table

For an original character, write one filmable action at the beginning, middle, and end. Record belief, strategy, and relationship for each. Decide whether the pattern is change, deterioration, recurrence, refusal to change, or uncertain, and cite evidence. “They learned to love” is not sufficient by itself.

### Exercise 3 | Compare AI options

Ask a local or otherwise authorized model for three strategies for the same scene. Do not use a paid service unless account access and budget are separately authorized. Preserve input, output, date, model version, and selection rationale; assess fit with known information, meaningful difference, unmarked invention, and bias.

**Further research:** Compare theories of character action and narrative causality; study how performance methods change viewers' attributions; examine how ensemble hierarchies distribute information; and investigate measures of character consistency in AI-generated text. Do not treat variables in a computational model as a complete account of audience psychology or artistic value.

## Conclusion
Character analysis helps a production team discuss choices, resistance, relationships, and consequences; it should not turn people into seemingly precise psychological labels. The next chapter moves from character action to scene beats, dialogue, and subtext.
