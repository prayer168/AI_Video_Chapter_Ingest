# On-Set Directing: Rehearsal, Coverage, and Real-Time Adjustment

## Scope and status

This chapter examines directing decisions from rehearsal through production: how actor action, blocking, coverage, and time constraints serve a shared dramatic purpose. Here, **coverage** means obtaining enough shot material to construct a scene in editing; it does not mean filming every possible angle without a rationale. Workflows vary by production scale, labor agreements, region, genre, performer needs, and directorial method. The process below is an adaptable model, not a mandatory standard.

<a id="CH15-S01"></a>
## Learning objectives
By the end of this chapter, readers should be able to:

1. Translate a scene objective into observable actor actions, turns, and audiovisual questions.
2. Explain dependencies among rehearsal, blocking, camera design, and coverage.
3. Select a limited, purposeful shot set based on dramatic information and editorial needs.
4. Preserve core dramatic intent during on-set changes while documenting revisions and continuity risks.
5. Manage AI assistance through traceable records and human judgment, without treating generated proposals as set instructions.

Prerequisites: scene, shot scale, axis of action, eyeline match, action continuity, and blocking. See CH02, CH13, and CH14 for related foundations.

<a id="CH15-S02"></a>
## Prerequisites
Read CH02 (shots and viewing position), CH13 (space, movement, and eyelines), and CH14 (storyboards and previsualization) first. Directing on set is not an isolated act of “managing performances”: safety, locations, schedules, performer consent, camera feasibility, sound, and editorial handoff all affect a workable plan. Stunts, intimate scenes, minors, hazardous props, and high-risk environments require procedures set by qualified personnel and applicable local rules. This chapter is not safety or legal advice.

## History and principles
Directorial responsibilities differ across production systems. ScreenSkills’ UK screen-industry role profile describes work that can include developing a visual approach and collaborating with performers and departments; it also notes that not every director rehearses in the same way [R107]. The profile is UK industry information, not a global contract, authority map, or fixed set procedure.

Rehearsal need not lock a performance. It can help a team discover problems earlier: whether an intention can be played, an action reads clearly, a space can be filmed, or the camera can see necessary information. Interviews in *American Cinematographer* describe individual practitioners’ experiences connecting rehearsal, visual storytelling, and shot decisions [R108–R110]. These are practitioner accounts, not universal production research. The 2003 Dublin Declaration published by the DGA records a professional-organizations’ statement about directors’ creative role [R111]; it is an advocacy document, not law, a global contract term, or proof of authority on every set.

A practical principle is to define what the audience should know, feel, or misunderstand before choosing actor actions and camera positions. Obtain the minimum coverage that is sufficient for the edit, and record reasons and consequences when plans change. This is a production recommendation in this book, not a claim presented as a research law.

<a id="CH15-S05"></a>
## Traditional production practice
### From dramatic problem to playable action

The director, writer, performers, and departments clarify a scene’s surface task, each character’s objective, obstacles, tactics, and turning point. Avoid giving only abstract notes such as “make it more tense.” Translate them into observable actions—delay an answer, break eye contact, handle an object before responding—then confirm with the performer that the action fits the character, scene, and acting method.

### Rehearsal and blocking

Rehearsal can range from table reads and dialogue-rhythm work to open exploration or specific blocking. Performers can first understand the situation and relationships; spatial constraints, props, and camera can be introduced when useful. Explain in advance whether a rehearsal will be open, recorded, or documented, and the scope of consent for that use. If blocking changes, camera, lighting, sound, and relevant production or safety personnel need a consistent update.

### Building coverage

A master, two-shot, single, reaction, or insert is an available tool, not a checklist. Ask what information each shot adds: spatial relationships, a response, a shift in power, a key prop, or an editorial connection. Reconsider a shot whose purpose is unclear. Conversely, coverage that is too sparse may prevent a performance from unfolding fully or force awkward edit points.

### Collaboration and safety on set

The director should communicate priorities clearly without allowing creative urgency to replace safety procedures, performer consent, or departmental expertise. Shooting order may differ from story order; the schedule, shot list, call sheet, and media labels should identify the current version. Local labor rules, union agreements, insurance, and production contracts should be checked by the producer and qualified advisers.

<a id="CH15-S06"></a>
## AI-era workflow and limits
AI can help organize an authorized script scene into candidate beats, character-objective tables, draft shot lists, continuity fields, or meeting notes. It can also turn a director’s dictation into a proposed change log. It cannot determine a performer’s mental state, consent, physical safety, on-set authority, or the artistically “correct” performance. Do not collect or process a performer’s likeness, voice, biometric data, or confidential script without authorization.

A useful pattern is **human decision, AI draft, traceable sources and versions**. Limit inputs to authorized, necessary script material and a clear task. Label the model and version, date, prompt, and human edits. The director and relevant departments approve each item. A generated video previsualization is a communication model, not automatic proof of feasibility, safety clearance, or final performance design.

<a id="CH15-S07"></a>
## Step-by-step workflow
1. **Define the scene problem:** Record audience information, character objectives, obstacles, and the turn, with script version and scene/page identifiers.
2. **Prepare communication:** Confirm participants, space, props, constraints, confidentiality, and consent. Assign high-risk action design to qualified personnel.
3. **Explore performance:** Use table reading, rhythm exploration, or improvisation as appropriate to the director’s method. Record useful findings without treating one rehearsal as the only answer.
4. **Test blocking:** Mark starting points, paths, stops, eyelines, and prop interactions; check feasibility and safety.
5. **Propose camera hypotheses:** Select a master and necessary additional shots based on narrative information and emotional distance. State each shot’s purpose.
6. **Align departments:** The director and camera, assistant directing, lighting, sound, art, and relevant personnel confirm order, equipment, time, and dependencies. Crew roles vary by location and production.
7. **Assess changes:** When conditions change, record the reason, indispensable story information, alternatives, and effects on safety, continuity, light, sound, and editorial options.
8. **Update and version:** Revise blocking diagrams and shot lists with version, time, decision-maker, and recipients. Keep superseded plans in the change record, separate from current set instructions.
9. **Review footage:** Confirm usable actions, lines, eyelines, reactions, and edit connections with script supervision and editorial needs; do not judge sufficiency by shot count alone.
10. **Handoff to editorial:** Deliver reports, continuity notes, selective director notes, and unresolved issues so the reasoning behind on-set choices is not lost.

## Prompt and technical parameters
The following is a tool-neutral prompt template and illustrative example. Do not present an invented scene as historical fact or a real production record.

```text
Task: Create a “rehearsal and coverage discussion draft” from scene text I am authorized to process. Do not make directing decisions.
Scene identifier: {project / script version / scene}
Scene objective and turn: {provided by user}
Character objectives / obstacles: {one per character}
Known space and constraints: {dimensions, exits, props, light, sound, or safety constraints; label unknowns}
Output fields:
1. Observable actor-action hypotheses (no psychological diagnosis)
2. Blocking and questions for departments
3. Candidate shots and the narrative purpose of each
4. Continuity and editorial risks
5. Safety, consent, privacy, and authorization items to confirm
6. Uncertainty, missing information, and source locators
Limits: Do not add plot facts not supplied. Do not claim approval by a director, performer, or safety professional.
Output version: {model/version / date / prompt version / human editor}
```

These are not universal model settings. Record the specific tool, model/version, date, prompt, context length, temperature if controllable, output format, and test results. Camera parameters (focal length, aperture, focus, camera height, movement speed) must be tested against actual lenses, locations, and equipment; language-model suggestions do not replace camera-department tests.

## Shot / film case study
### Case 1: Rehearsal as a department-information exchange

ScreenSkills’ UK director profile describes responsibilities that may include rehearsal and collaboration with performers and departments [R107]. Use it to generate questions: who convenes the rehearsal on this production? Which departments attend? When is blocking confirmed? Answer these from the actual crew plan, contracts, and call sheets; do not infer them from a role profile alone.

### Case 2: Rehearsal and camera decisions

Practitioner interviews in *American Cinematographer* document cinematographer-directors discussing their own experiences of rehearsal, visual storytelling, and shot planning [R108–R110]. Compare approaches such as “rehearse fully before designing coverage” and “let camera participate in exploration,” while attributing each account to its own context. No legal viewing copy and scene locator have yet been verified for a specific film, so this chapter does not claim that a particular shot or timecode proves a general principle.

### Practice scene (illustrative, not a film case)

Two characters exchange a key in a narrow corridor; one changes their mind at the last moment. List the information the audience must see, then propose one spatially inclusive shot and up to three additional shots. Explain the unique purpose of each. Design a revised version in case performer blocking or available shooting time changes. This hypothetical exercise does not refer to a specific film.

## Common errors and quality checks
| Error | Possible consequence | Check / response |
|---|---|---|
| Treating rehearsal as a demand to reproduce a fixed emotion | Rigid performance; disregards actor method and consent | Communicate circumstances, objectives, and playable actions; retain room to explore |
| Shooting many angles because they “might be useful” | Time and resource loss without new editorial information | State each shot’s purpose and review redundant shots before filming |
| Too little coverage without checking performance integrity | Missing reactions, action connections, or edit points | Check scene beats and required reactions; shot count is not a substitute for judgment |
| Changing blocking without updating departments | Mismatch in lighting, sound, focus, or continuity | Use one current blocking/shot-list version and confirm recipients |
| Failing to record why a plan changed | Editorial cannot understand material differences | Log reason, decision-maker, version, alternatives, and risks |
| AI invents action, safety, or character information | Unsafe or incorrect execution; privacy risk | Trace inputs, label unknowns, and require human approval |

Acceptance checks: scene objective and turn are clear; principal blocking is understandable; every candidate shot has a purpose; change plans include dependencies and continuity effects; safety and consent matters have an appropriate owner; footage can be traced by scene and shot identifiers.

<a id="CH15-S11"></a>
## Exercises, assessment, and research
1. Convert a dialogue scene into beats, marking objectives, tactical changes, and information the audience needs.
2. Design a “master-first” and a “coverage-based” option for the same scene; explain the trade-offs for performance, time, and editing.
3. Simulate a location-driven blocking change; update the shot list and change log, naming departments to notify and continuity checks.
4. Use the prompt template to create an AI draft; mark each item as usable, requiring verification, or outside AI decision-making.
5. Research: compare at least two director-workflow sources with identifiable jurisdiction and authorship. Test whether the claim “rehearsal is always necessary” is supported. Do not generalize an individual interview into industry consensus.

Assess traceability, dramatic purpose, shot purpose, completeness of collaboration information, and handling of safety and consent. Aesthetic judgment requires human review; an automated score must not be presented as an objective verdict.
